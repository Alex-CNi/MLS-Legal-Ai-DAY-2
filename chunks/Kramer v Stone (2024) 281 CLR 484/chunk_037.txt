described by Dixon J in Thompson v Palmer (98), equitable estoppels
could arise from “encouragement to adhere to an assumption or
expectation already formed” or from acquiescence which is “the result
of a party’s failure to object to the assumption or expectation on which
the other party is known to be conducting [their] affairs” (99).
In assessing whether a person who is subject to an estoppel bears
“responsibility for the detrimental reliance” of the other party (100),
there is a signiﬁcant difference between a person whose promise causes
another’s detriment and a person who merely omits to act where action
could spare the other party from detriment. Any responsibility in the
latter case must require the omission to occur with knowledge of that
person’s rights and of the other’s mistake so as to give rise to a duty
to speak (101). By contrast, there is no justiﬁcation for such a
requirement in the former case. As Leeming JA said of estoppel by
encouragement in his concurring reasons in the Court of Appeal in this
case (with which Kirk JA also agreed) (102):
“Why should it be necessary not only to know that the defendant
has encouraged the plaintiff to labour under a false belief, but also
to know that the plaintiff has relied on the encouragement? The
distinction is quite artiﬁcial. Further, I can see no reason why two
landowners, both of whom make the same representation to their
neighbours who act upon it, should be in different positions if one is
thereafter absent from the country and has no means of knowing
what steps have been taken by the neighbour.”
